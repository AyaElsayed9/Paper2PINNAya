Periodic Boundary Conditions in Generalized Beam Theory: A Shear-Deformable GBT–Ritz Formulation for the Buckling of Omega-Stringer-Stiffened Composite Panels

::: {custom-style="MDPI_1.7_abstract"}
**Abstract:** Stringer-stiffened composite panels in aircraft covers carry many identical stringers, so that their buckling behaviour is commonly analysed on one representative cell whose side edges satisfy periodic boundary conditions. Closed-form models with periodic conditions rely on idealisations of the skin–stringer interaction, while shell finite element models with periodic constraint equations are accurate but expensive and give no insight into the deformation mechanisms. Generalized Beam Theory (GBT) provides such insight at low cost, but its cross-section analysis has been developed for members with free longitudinal edges or closed contours, and periodic side edges have not been treated. This study combines GBT and the Ritz method into a shear-deformable GBT–Ritz formulation for omega-stringer-stiffened composite panels in which the periodic condition is built into every family of deformation modes: the conventional modes through constraint rows that are projected out family by family, the warping functions through a constrained Vlasov least-squares problem, and the shear and transverse-shear modes through merging of the nodal functions of the two side edges. Every mode, and therefore every truncated mode basis, satisfies the periodic condition exactly. The walls are laminated Reissner–Mindlin plates with full membrane–bending and anisotropic coupling, the stringer is tied to the skin with its true offset, the pre-buckling state is the first-order solution under the actual end loads, and the amplitude functions are cubic B-splines, which cover simply supported and clamped loaded edges. The modal tensors, equilibrium equations and boundary conditions are derived in the notation of the shear-deformable GBT of Silvestre and Camotim, and the formulation reduces to the classical GBT buckling equations for uncoupled walls under uniform compression. For 30 panels with simply supported loaded edges (five lay-ups, two stringer pitches, three aspect ratios), the critical loads agree with Abaqus shell models with periodic constraint equations within 0.57 % on average and 1.25 % at most@@ABS_CC@@. For cells up to three stringer pitches long, an energy-based mode decomposition identifies all critical modes as local skin buckling with global and distortional contributions below 4 %. Longer cells change their buckling mode: cross-ply and unsymmetric cells buckle globally, whereas isotropic and angle-ply cells, whose skins have a large Poisson ratio, buckle in a long-wave skin–stringer mode that is driven by the transverse compression induced by the constant width of the periodic cell and lowers the critical load by a factor of up to @@FACTOR@@ compared with the same cell without this compression.

# Introduction

Stringer-stiffened panels are the standard construction of aircraft fuselage and wing covers, and their buckling behaviour is often the governing design criterion of weight-critical laminated composite structures {{zhang2024,mittelstedt2025book}}. Among the stiffener shapes, the omega (hat) stringer is attractive because its closed cell provides high bending and torsional stiffness, a wide bonding area and good damage tolerance {{mittelstedtbeerhorst2009,schilling2020tws}}. A typical cover carries many identical stringers. Its buckling behaviour away from the free or supported longitudinal edges is therefore represented by one repeating cell, provided that the side edges of this cell are given the conditions that the neighbouring cells impose, namely periodic boundary conditions {{mittelstedt2007,mittelstedt2009periodic,schilling2020rineng}}.

The treatment of the repeating cell is a classical topic of the analysis of stiffened panels. In exact and finite strip methods for prismatic plate assemblies, the infinitely wide panel was analysed early through its repeating portion: the exact strip programs VIPASA and VICONOPT treat infinitely wide or long panels by repeating a portion and its supports {{wittrick1974,kennedy1994}}, and the finite strip method in general handles such assemblies with a small number of strips {{cheung1976,lau1986}}. The underlying principle is the theory of waves in periodic structures (Bloch–Floquet theory), by which the response of an infinite periodic structure follows from one cell with phase-related boundary conditions {{mead1996}}.

At the Institute for Lightweight Engineering and Structural Mechanics (LSM), a series of closed-form and semi-analytical models for stringer-stiffened composite panels with periodic boundary conditions has been developed. Mittelstedt derived closed-form buckling loads of blade-stringer-stiffened composite plates considering periodic boundary conditions {{mittelstedt2007}} and extended the approach to the stiffener–plate interaction in local buckling {{mittelstedt2009periodic}}. For omega stringers, Mittelstedt and Beerhorst presented a closed-form buckling analysis {{mittelstedtbeerhorst2009}}, and Mittelstedt and Schröder treated the local postbuckling of hat-stringer-stiffened plates {{mittelstedtschroder2010}}. Schilling and Mittelstedt developed a closed-form approximate solution for the local buckling of omega-stringer-stiffened panels {{schilling2020tws}}, a column-buckling model with periodic boundary conditions {{schilling2020rineng}}, and models for shear buckling, compression–shear interaction and local postbuckling {{schilling2021shear,schilling2022interaction,schilling2022post}}. Most recently, El Yaakoubi-Mesbah and Mittelstedt presented a closed-form analytical solution for the local buckling of omega-stringer-stiffened composite panels under compression {{elyaakoubi2025}}, a computational model for local buckling {{elyaakoubi2024wccm}} and a closed-form postbuckling analysis including the stringer-foot effects {{elyaakoubi2026}}; the stability of stiffened laminates is summarised in {{mittelstedt2025stiff}}. These models are efficient and transparent, but they rest on idealisations: the stringer is replaced by elastic restraints, the skin is reduced to one or a few plate strips with assumed shape functions, local and global buckling are usually treated by separate models, and the pre-buckling stress is taken as uniform. The membrane–bending coupling of unsymmetric laminates is typically included through a reduced bending stiffness {{ewing1988}}. The physics-informed neural network of the authors for the local buckling of omega-stringer-stiffened panels {{elsayed2026}} was built on such a reduced energy model. Shell finite element models with periodic constraint equations between the nodes of the two side edges, on the other hand, are accurate and general, but each design requires a new mesh and a large eigenvalue analysis, and the buckling mode is obtained only as a nodal field, without a decomposition into mechanically meaningful deformation mechanisms.

Generalized Beam Theory (GBT), originated by Schardt {{schardt1989,schardt1994}} and developed further by Davies and Leach {{davies1994}}, describes the deformation of a prismatic thin-walled member as a superposition of cross-section deformation modes whose amplitudes vary along the member axis. A cross-section analysis determines the modes and the associated modal matrices, and a member analysis determines the amplitude functions. The modal decomposition separates global, distortional, local and shear-related deformations and has made GBT one of the most transparent tools for coupled instabilities of thin-walled members, as summarised in the state-of-the-art reports by Camotim et al. {{camotim2010}} and Mittelstedt {{mittelstedt2020amr,mittelstedt2024gbt,mittelstedt2025gbtch}}. Silvestre and Camotim formulated first- and second-order GBT for arbitrary orthotropic materials {{silvestre2002a,silvestre2002b}}, and later shear-deformable GBT for composite members and its vibration counterpart {{silvestre2013shear,silvestre2013vib}}. Dinis et al. extended the cross-section analysis to arbitrarily branched open sections {{dinis2006}}. Gonçalves et al. introduced a new approach to the calculation of the cross-section deformation modes {{goncalves2010}}, and Bebiano et al. rationalised the cross-section analysis into natural, shear and transverse-extension mode families {{bebiano2015}}, together with the shear deformation modes {{goncalves2014shear}}, implemented in the code GBTUL 2.0 {{bebiano2018}}. Closed sections were treated, among others, for circular cylindrical shells and tubes {{silvestre2007cyl}} and for regular polygonal tubes {{goncalves2013polygon}}. For laminated composites, GBT has been applied to FRP open-section columns {{silva2010}} and to laminated CFRP cylindrical stiffened panels {{silva2010ijssd}}, and Taig and Ranzi formulated GBT for stiffened sections {{taig2014}}.

The Ritz method provides the complementary tool for the member analysis: the continuous buckling problem is replaced by a finite-dimensional one over admissible functions that satisfy the essential boundary conditions {{mittelstedt2025ritz}}, and its efficiency depends on a basis that reflects the expected solution {{vescovini2018}}. Outside GBT, Kühn et al. used such variational discrete-plate models for the local buckling of shear-deformable laminated beams {{kuehn2014}}. Kharghani and Mittelstedt combined GBT and the Ritz method into a GBT–Ritz approach: for global, local and distortional buckling of shear-deformable composite beams with open cross-sections {{kharghani2025jcs}}, for composite box girders {{kharghani2025semc}}, and for thin-walled composite beams with unsymmetric layups, for which the membrane–bending coupling is consistently included {{kharghani2026cepa,kharghani2026jcs}}. In this approach GBT defines the kinematics through the modes, and the Ritz method determines how the modal amplitudes vary along the member.

Two gaps motivate the present study. The first gap lies in GBT. GBT has been developed for members: open sections with free longitudinal edges, branched sections, and closed contours. The skin of a stiffened panel cell, however, ends at two side edges that are neither free nor closed: the displacement field at one side edge must equal that at the other. This condition has not been formulated in GBT, and it cannot simply be imposed afterwards on a set of classical modes. Classical mode generation starts from a few natural nodes, whereas the periodic condition couples the two ends of the open contour; a truncated set of classical modes then contains too few modes that satisfy it, and the rigid-body and warping modes of the open cell are not admissible for the periodic cell. In addition, the omega stringer forms closed cells with the skin, so that Vlasov's condition of null membrane shear strain cannot be fulfilled by the warping functions, the stringer is offset from the skin by the half thicknesses, and the pre-buckling state is not uniform near the loaded edges, where the load enters the stringer through the eccentric foot. The second gap lies in the treatment of periodic conditions. The closed-form models are efficient, but they rely on idealisations of the skin–stringer interaction, separate local and global buckling, and do not account for the non-uniform pre-buckling state; shell finite element models do not have these restrictions, but they are expensive for parametric studies and give no modal insight; exact and finite strip methods treat the repeating portion efficiently, but they are formulated in nodal degrees of freedom and do not provide the decomposition into global, distortional and local mechanisms.

GBT and periodic boundary conditions complement each other. The periodic cell is prismatic, so that the member analysis of GBT applies directly; the modal decomposition identifies the buckling mechanisms of the cell; and the hierarchical structure of the modes is the basis for reduced mode sets. The present study combines them. Its novelty is the formulation of the periodic condition within the cross-section analysis of GBT, such that every deformation mode, and therefore every combination and truncation of the modes, satisfies it exactly, together with a shear-deformable GBT–Ritz member analysis of omega-stringer-stiffened panels with symmetric and unsymmetric laminates. The formulation is derived in the notation of the shear-deformable GBT of Silvestre and Camotim {{silvestre2013shear}}: the modal tensors, equilibrium equations and boundary conditions are given explicitly, extended by the membrane terms that the closed contours of the cell require, and solved by the GBT–Ritz approach {{kharghani2026jcs}}; the formulation reduces to the classical GBT buckling equations for uncoupled walls under uniform compression. The formulation is verified against Abaqus shell models with periodic constraint equations for simply supported and clamped loaded edges. The mode decomposition is then used to follow the change of the buckling mode with the length of the cell, which reveals, besides the transition from local to global buckling, a long-wave skin–stringer mode that is caused by the constant width imposed by the periodic condition.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 develops the formulation: the cross-section analysis of the periodic cell, the periodic boundary conditions, the member analysis by the Ritz method and the mode decomposition. Section 3 presents and discusses the results, and Section 4 summarises the conclusions.
