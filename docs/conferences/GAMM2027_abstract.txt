Title: Physics-informed neural networks with a certified Generalized Beam Theory check for the buckling of stiffened composite panels
Section: S25 Machine Learning and Data Science in Applied Mathematics and Mechanics (alternative: S04 Structural Mechanics)
Keywords: physics-informed neural networks; Generalized Beam Theory; buckling; stiffened composite panels; a posteriori error estimate; reduced-order model

Design studies of stiffened composite panels need the buckling load of many variants. Finite element models are accurate but slow. Data-driven models are fast, but they need many training results and cannot tell how wrong a prediction is.

In earlier work, we predicted the local buckling of omega-stringer panels with a hybrid physics-informed neural network (PINN). Its physics was a simplified plate model, and suspicious predictions were checked with Abaqus. In this work, Abaqus is replaced by our periodic Generalized Beam Theory (GBT) code, and the new PINN is built entirely on it: GBT gives the energy the network learns from, the results it is trained and tested against, and the check of every prediction. At the Abaqus points of the earlier study, GBT differs from Abaqus by 0.54 % on average, compared with 2.53 % for the earlier PINN.

To let one network handle many designs, we build the buckling modes of every design from the same pieces: a fixed set of cross-section shapes and a fixed set of functions along the length. The network takes the stringer pitch and the panel aspect ratio and predicts the buckling load and eight candidate buckling modes. It learns by minimizing the GBT energy ratio (Rayleigh quotient) of its predicted modes. At first the network did not train, because the problem was badly conditioned. Unknowns scaled by the strain energy and a natural-gradient training step solved this. We then compared three losses: physics only, GBT results only, and both. Physics alone gave the best mode shapes, but its load was too high; adding GBT results removed this bias. A single network for all lay-ups stopped improving at about 5 % error, and so did standard data-driven models. We therefore trained one small network for each lay-up.

The predicted modes are the starting point of a short GBT eigenvalue iteration (LOBPCG) that checks the answer. Every step gives an upper bound of the buckling load of the reduced model, which is within 0.3 % of full GBT. The remaining residual gives an error estimate that needs no reference result.

For four lay-ups and two lateral boundary conditions (320 unseen designs), the networks trained with 120 GBT results per lay-up predict the buckling load instantly with mean errors of 0.24 to 1.18 %. Without any GBT results, they learn from the physics alone and their modes still start the check well. The error estimate was never smaller than the true error in 3800 cases. A 5 % target is met on all designs without any GBT results, in about 7 to 8 s per design instead of 11 s for a full GBT solution. A Gaussian process needed 80 GBT results per lay-up for the same target and gives neither a guarantee nor the mode shapes. Compared with simple alternatives, the network's modes save about two iterations of the check, no more than copying the modes of the nearest computed design. The network gives an instant answer with physical mode shapes, and the GBT check certifies it.
