Physics-informed neural networks with a certified Generalized Beam Theory check for the buckling of stiffened composite panels

===== ABSTRACT =====
Omega-stringer composite panels are common in aircraft, and their design is often governed by local skin buckling. Design studies therefore need the buckling load of many variants. Shell finite element models are accurate but costly, and data-driven models need many training results and cannot tell how wrong a prediction is. Our earlier physics-informed neural network (PINN) used a simplified plate model as physics and checked doubtful predictions with Abaqus.

In this work, we build a new PINN entirely on Generalized Beam Theory (GBT), which replaces both the plate model and Abaqus. GBT describes the cross-section deformation by a few modes, each multiplied by a function along the length. Our periodic GBT formulation agrees with Abaqus within 1.6 % in 60 test cases.

The method has three steps. First, we describe all designs with the same unknowns, so that one network can serve them all: cross-section shapes obtained once from the GBT modes of 64 sample panels, combined with fixed sine waves and end functions along the length. This changes the buckling load by at most 0.3 %. Second, we train one network per lay-up. From the stringer pitch and the panel aspect ratio, the network returns an instant buckling load and eight candidate modes; its decoder builds the cross-section part of each length function, so that the buckle pattern can follow the design. We train it on 152 designs by minimizing the GBT energy ratio (Rayleigh quotient) of its modes, alone or with up to 120 GBT results. The training succeeded only after we scaled the unknowns by the strain energy and began each round with one GBT iteration step per design. Third, we check every prediction: the predicted modes start a block eigenvalue iteration (LOBPCG) on the GBT matrices of the design. Each step gives an upper bound of the buckling load in these unknowns, and the relative residual estimates the error without a reference solution. The iteration stops when this estimate falls below the tolerance; if it does not within 40 steps, the design is solved with full GBT.

One network for all lay-ups had maximum errors above 20 %, like standard data-driven models. For four lay-ups and two lateral boundary conditions, we tested 320 unseen designs. With 120 GBT results per lay-up, the networks predicted the buckling load instantly within 4.9 % of full GBT; trained on physics alone, they overestimated the load but still gave useful starting modes. The error estimate was never smaller than the true error in 3800 checks. For a 5 % tolerance, the iteration needed 3 to 15 steps and no design needed full GBT; without GBT results, the largest error was 2.5 %, at 7 to 8 s per design instead of 11 s. A Gaussian process needed 80 GBT results per lay-up for this tolerance and gave no guarantee. At the 22 Abaqus points of the earlier study, the new PINN with the check deviated from Abaqus by at most 1.19 %, against 10.80 % for the earlier PINN.
